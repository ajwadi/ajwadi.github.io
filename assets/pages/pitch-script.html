Questions you will get

Answers kept short on purpose. The long version loses the room.

So the AI made six mistakes?
Yes. And we caught all six in under a second, before anyone used them. Every engineering process produces errors — the question is whether they're caught before release. This one has a measured catch rate. Our hand-built spreadsheets don't have one at all, because nothing checks them. Say this before they do, in the trust beat, and it never comes up as an attack.
Who signs it off? What about liability?
The same engineer who signs today. This doesn't change who approves anything. It changes how long the first ninety per cent takes. Every tool says on its face that it's a first-order sizing aid and to check against the governing standard before release.
Why not just buy KISSsoft, MITCalc or use Inventor?
We should, for what they're good at — they're mature and validated and I'd rather have them for gearboxes. They cost per seat and they solve the problems someone else decided were worth solving. This is for the fifty calculations specific to how we work that nobody will ever sell us software for. It's not either/or.
Is it actually accurate?
Press the button yourself — it checks in front of you, every time you open it. Stress areas match the ISO table to within 0.4 per cent across all eleven sizes. And it's a sizing aid, not a replacement for the standard. I'd use it to get to a candidate design in an hour instead of a day, then verify the candidate the way we verify anything.
What can't it do?
Three things I'd want before trusting it on a real release: transverse load and joint slip, fatigue, and thermal preload loss across a mixed-material stack. It also handles one bolt, not a bolt pattern. Knowing exactly where the edges are is part of why I trust the middle.
Could anyone here do this, or just you?
The describing part, anyone who knows the engineering can do. The reviewing part is the job — someone has to know that a nylon washer under a preloaded bolt is a problem, and that's not something the tool teaches you. That's the honest limit, and it's why I'm asking for a pilot team rather than a licence for everyone.

Before you walk in

  • Record a backup. Ninety seconds of the add-layer and nylon-washer moments. Live demos die.
  • Open all three files in tabs and press Run checks once, so the first load is already warm.
  • Load a real joint from a real project instead of the sample. "Here's the bracket from [project]" beats "here's an example" by a mile.
  • Fill in every red blank. Get the hourly rate from someone who knows it rather than estimating on stage.
  • Decide your ask in one sentence and be able to say it without looking down.
  • Switch to Basic mode if the room is mostly non-technical — fewer fields, same four numbers.
  • Don't type live. Click things that already work. Typing on stage always goes wrong.
